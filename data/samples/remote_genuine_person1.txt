When conflict comes up in a project team, my first step is to work out what is really going on before I try to fix anything. In my experience people often argue about the surface issue, like who is responsible for which task, while the real problem sits underneath. Someone might feel that their contribution isn't being recognised, or they might be under pressure from other deadlines.

Because of that, I'd start by speaking to each person on their own. I think it's important that people feel heard, and a one-on-one chat is much less threatening than being put on the spot in front of the group. I'd ask what they see as the problem and what a good outcome would look like for them. At this stage I wouldn't take sides. My job is mostly to listen.

After that I would bring everyone together to agree on a way forward. In my second year, our Organisational Behaviour group had two members who almost stopped speaking because one kept rewriting the slides the night before we presented. It got quite tense. What finally helped was sitting down and drawing up a simple plan - who owns each section, and a cut-off time for changes. Once the expectations were written down, there was far less to argue about.

I also try to be honest about my own role. I know I can be a bit controlling when I'm worried about a deadline, so I'd check whether I am adding to the tension rather than calming it down. It's easy to blame other people and forget that you're part of the team too.

If the situation still didn't improve, I would involve the lecturer or the manager, but only once we had properly tried to sort it out ourselves. I don't think conflict is always a bad thing. Disagreements can push a team towards a better idea, as long as people stay respectful and keep the focus on the work rather than on each other.

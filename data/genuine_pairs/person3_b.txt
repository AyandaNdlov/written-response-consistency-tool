The most effective team I have been part of was a group of four students who entered a case competition in our third year, and what made it work was not that we were the most talented group (we were not) but that we were honest about our strengths early on. In our first meeting each of us described what we were good at and, more usefully, what we tended to avoid; I admitted that I procrastinate on anything involving numbers, while one of my teammates confessed that she hated presenting.

That conversation shaped how we divided the work. Rather than splitting the case into four equal parts, we organised it around those strengths: I took the stakeholder analysis and the written recommendations, our strongest analyst built the financial model, and the two others shared the presentation, with the nervous presenter handling the question-and-answer section, which she preferred because it felt more like a conversation.

Of course, it was not entirely smooth. Two days before the final round we realised that the financial model and my recommendations did not quite agree, and there was a tense evening in the library while we argued about which one to change. What resolved it was going back to the client brief together and asking which option actually answered the question they had asked; once we did that, the disagreement became a shared problem rather than a personal one.

We placed second, which was a pleasant surprise, but the more lasting lesson for me was that a team works best when people feel safe admitting their weaknesses.

Ethical dilemmas at work are rarely as dramatic as the textbook cases suggest; more often they are small, quiet decisions that nobody else will notice, which is exactly what makes them difficult. During my internship at a market research firm I was asked to "clean" a survey dataset before it went to a client, and while most of the cleaning was routine (removing duplicates, fixing obvious typing errors), my team lead also suggested removing a group of responses that pulled the satisfaction score down.

Her reasoning was that those respondents had rushed through the survey, and to be fair, some of them had. However, when I looked more closely, the completion times for that group were not very different from the rest of the sample, and removing them would have changed the headline result from "mixed" to "positive". I was uncomfortable, partly because I was the most junior person in the room and partly because I could see why she wanted a cleaner story for the client.

In the end I prepared two versions of the analysis, one with and one without the questionable responses, and I wrote a short note explaining the criteria I had used. I took this to her rather than to her manager, because I wanted to give her the chance to make the call herself. She agreed to send the full version with a footnote. I learnt that raising a concern is easier when you bring evidence and an alternative, not just an objection.
